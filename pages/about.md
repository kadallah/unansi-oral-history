# Overview

Many of Western society’s problems have arisen out of an orientation towards centralization. Capitalism, vectoralism, colonization, fascism, hetero-normative patriarchy are all built around and in service of centralizing resources and power behind a few select people. The political turmoil currently facing the United States in particular is a great case in point: we have a presidential administration actively dismantling barriers to the consolidation of political power as well as deregulation of private industry to allow for more concentration of wealth. Because of the domination of Western culture around the world, this orientation towards centralization has been taken for granted as a universal truth for humankind writ large. Despite how recently Garrett Hardin’s Tragedy of the Commons was written, it has been largely accepted as fact: people are naturally selfish and if left to their own devices will work to hoard resources for themselves. But, this truism is not inherently true. This tendency towards centralization is the fruit of a specific philosophical tradition, but one that due to this particular bias seeks to negate all other contrary ideologies.

However, this partiality towards centralization, or even “philosophy” as a guiding force, is not inherent to all people. When we look at contemporary and ancient African and diasporic communities, we find instead that Black societies seemingly favor a praxis of distribution over philosophies of centralization. We see this across culture, geographic distance, and time: from the foundational Bantu concept of Ubuntu (”I am because we are”), to the “African socialist” political ideology of Ujamaa, to FUBU—both the brand and business sense from which the former gets its name of “For Us, By Us”—Black cultures place more value in ensuring that entire communities are supported rather than the consolidation of resources. What’s more, this ideology is not simply a response to colonization and White supremacy, nor is it just a collection of abstract concepts and pithy platitudes. In the essay Africa as a Success Story: Political Organization in Pre-Colonial Africa, Soeren J. Henn and James A. Robinson provide evidence that prior to contact with the West, Africa likely had a network of over 44,000 multi-ethnic polities that were explicitly organized to distribute power and curb the process of centralization. Their research, much like this oral history project makes the argument that Africans did not fail at a philosophy of centralization, we succeeded at a praxis of distribution.

These examples not only contrast with a bias towards centralization, they defy Western approaches to philosophy. While the Western world—reactionaries and conservatives in particular—have a fascination with upholding and maintaining an unbroken lineage of Western thought as transmitted through written text (a medium that preserves ideas in amber, unchanged through context), Black practices of distribution are better described as praxis than philosophy. These ways of being get their power not through remaining ideologically pure to an original philosopher’s words, but instead through the impact the actions have on the world. This orientation towards praxis over philosophy is in and of itself an embodiment of distribution: we don’t know the first person to coin the term “ubuntu” and as such, it is a concept that belongs to anyone that considers themselves a part of the “we;” the effectiveness of a People’s Free Food program, Citizenship School or chama isn’t measured by how ideologically pure an organizer is to the original creator’s vision, but by the impact the effort has on the community it is embedded within.

When thinking about this Black praxis of distribution, I have begun to use the term Unansi to encompass the range of this ideology. Distribution, decentralization, communalism, cooperativism all hint at these ideas, but it is important to acknowledge a cultural heritage that grounds this set of praxis. This isn’t simply Black cooperativism or African socialism, it is a school of thought that has explicitly arisen out of Black cultural contexts in contrast to praxis developed within the bounds of a society built around centralization. Unansi comes from African communities in which many people share resources despite diverse kabila (ethnic backgrounds), from Caribbean maroon communities that needed to be self-reliant when existing outside of Babylonian law, and diasporic Black communities that learned to thrive through interdependent networks of support across hostile landscapes.

Unansi as a term combines the Akan figure of Anansi with the Bantu conjugation for concepts (like ubuntu or ujamaa). In the West, Anansi is primarily known as a trickster figure but their legacy stands for much more than mischief. Originating out of the Akan story form of Anansesem in which the story teller is able to question the powers that be, as Anansi has spread first through West Africa and then through the diaspora, they have come to be known as a weaver of webs and a breaker of chains. Anansi does not only represent cunning, but is a symbol of resistance and a shared cultural touch stone for Black people all over the world. Using the Bantu “U-” conjugation both signals that this is an ideology born of Black culture—which would be less obvious if using the English “-ism”—and acts as a bridge between West Africa where Anansi originated and Eastern, Central, and Southern Africa where Bantu languages and people are more common.

This oral history project seeks to expand the range of communities present in the Governance Ecologies repository of research. Through the project, we will further articulate Unansi as an explicit ideological formation by speaking to Black community builders across disciplines, cultural contexts, and geographic locations. This work will not just showcase Black communities in isolation, but instead posits that there is a throughline between our organizational practices. By establishing this throughline, the hope is that we can reorient our relationship to Blackness, whiteness, centralization, and liberation. We seek to show that Black communities are not just victims of white supremacy waiting to be liberated, but instead that we are the inheritors and stewards of a praxis of distribution that is necessary to liberate us all from the abuses of centralization.

# Methodology

## Oral History

This research will take the form of an oral history archive. Oral histories as a form of research feels uniquely well-suited for a project designed to articulate the initial conceptualization of a new ideological formation defined by cooperation. This methodology allows us to speak with real people about their experiences with these concepts and then define the ideology through the results of these conversations. Oral history as a research technique is known for the ways it humanizes participants and seeks out lived subjective experiences over analytical objective truth, making it a uniquely pluralistic method. Further, it only feels appropriate to conduct this research via oral history when oral storytelling traditions are at the heart of Unansi praxis.

### Questions

The questions that will be asked of interviewees will differ slightly from conversation to conversation, but will largely follow the same template in order to begin to weave together understanding across interviews.

First, we will establish base context through demographic questions about the interviewee and any relevant communities that they are a part of.

- Where are you from, and where are you located now (if different)?
- What holds your community together? What is it’s mission?

Second, we will try to understand the different types of Unansi praxis the interviewee employs within their communities across different domains, namely: governance, technological, financial, and social structures. These questions will mostly be specific to the interviewee and community being discussed; for example, a maintainer of an open source project may receive more questions about decentralization in their tech stack while a community organizer may share more about cooperative governance structures.

- How does cooperativism show up in your governance?
- What does open source mean to your organization?

Third, we will get into a more theoretical discussion about Blackness, praxis, and distribution. In this turn of the conversation, the goal is to tease out how interviewees understand specific concepts and their relationship to them.

- What does Blackness look like to you?
- What does decentralization mean in the context of your work?

Fourth, we will attempt to establish a lineage or connection between the praxis used by interviewees and their Black cultural heritage.

- Are their specific Black “truisms” that inform your work?
- Who and what communities inspire you in your work?

Fifth, we will seek to understand a wider network of Unansi communities by asking for recommendations for other communities and interviewees to speak with.

- Are there members of your community who work in similarly distributed ways?
- What work would you recommend I engage with around these topics?

These sections will not necessarily come in this specific order or be exclusively clumped together, but the goal will be to cover each of these lines of inquiry across all interviews.

## About Oral History as Data

The [Oral History as Data (OHD)](https://github.com/oralhistoryasdata/) framework is a static website generator that allows users to analyze and publish coded oral history or qualitative interview files. By turning transcriptions into tagged/coded CSV files, adding a list of filters, and creating a simple markdown file for each interview (to be included in the _transcripts collection), OHD will provide filterable transcripts and a color coded visualization for all transcripts included.
